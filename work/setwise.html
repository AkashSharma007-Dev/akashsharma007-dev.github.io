Setwise

A workout log for people who lift with a plan. It shows what to lift next, so the rest between sets goes on resting, not on scrolling back through old notes.

Platform
iPhone and Android

The problem

People who train with a written plan add a little weight when they hit their reps. To do that they need three facts in the middle of a workout: what they lifted last time, what to try today, and how long until the next set.

Many keep this in a notes app or on paper. General fitness apps hide it behind feeds, badges and menus. Between sets there are about two minutes, the hands are sweaty and the phone is often lying on a bench.

Who it's for

I designed for someone who trains three or four times a week from a plan, logs every set, and uses the phone with one hand between sets. These are my assumptions for the concept, not findings from research.

Three rules came from that:

  • A set that matches the plan logs with one tap.
  • Every action during a workout sits within reach of the thumb.
  • Weights and reps can be read at arm's length.

The flow

  1. Today's plan
  2. Log a set
  3. Rest timer
  4. Finish
  5. Summary

Steps 2 and 3 repeat for every set. Progress is one tap away from the tab bar at any time.

Wireframes

Grey boxes first, to settle what each screen must show and in what order before choosing any colour.

Design decisions

  1. Last time sits next to this time

    Each set row shows the previous numbers beside the fields for today's. Nobody has to leave the screen to remember what they did.

  2. The app does the sum, the lifter decides

    When every set of last week was completed, a hint suggests 2.5 kg more and fills it in with one tap. It never changes the plan on its own.

  3. The rest timer stays in view

    After a set, a slim bar counts down at the bottom of the logging screen. Tapping it opens a large timer with buttons to add or take off 15 seconds.

  4. Colours borrowed from the gym floor

    Exercises are marked in red, blue, yellow and green, the colours of standard weight plates. Lifters already know them, and they stay readable in a bright gym.

  5. Progress as strength, not only volume

    The progress chart plots estimated one-rep max, which rises when either the weight or the reps go up. Records are listed under it with their dates.

What I would test next

  1. Ask five people who lift to log a real session with the prototype on their own phone. Count taps per set and note every time they scroll back.
  2. Check whether the suggestion to add weight feels helpful or pushy, against a version that only shows the hint without the button.
  3. See whether the rest bar is noticed when the phone lies face down, and whether a vibration at zero is enough.
  4. Find out whether people understand "estimated one-rep max" or would rather see their heaviest set.

Setwise is a concept project. The app, its users and all figures are fictional.
