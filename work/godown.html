Stock control for a wholesale distributor, built around the question the owner asks every morning: what runs out first?

Godown, overview

The problem

The client in this concept is Mehta Distributors, a grocery wholesaler in Jaipur with one godown and about 400 products. Stock lives in the billing software and an Excel sheet.

Billing software reports how many cartons are left. The owner still has to work out how long that lasts, and whether more is already on order. Run out of a fast seller and shops buy from the next wholesaler. Order too much and cash sits in cartons for months.

Who it's for

Two people use it, on a desktop at the office counter. These are my assumptions for the concept, not findings from research.

  • The owner decides what to buy and watches the cash tied up in stock.
  • The godown manager receives deliveries and sends the oldest batches out first.

Sales and stock come in from the billing software, so nobody types numbers twice.

The flow

  1. Overview
  2. Reorder now
  3. Product detail
  4. New purchase order
  5. Send on WhatsApp

A second route: Products, filter by "Below reorder level", tick several products from one supplier, then create one purchase order for all of them.

Wireframes

The layout was settled in grey first: a fixed sidebar, a summary row, and the reorder list given the most space because it is where the day's work starts.

Design decisions

  1. Days, not cartons

    Every product shows how many days its stock will last, from current stock and the average daily sales. Fourteen cartons of mustard oil and 120 cartons of biscuits can now be compared at a glance: three days against eighteen.

  2. Show what is already on order

    The reorder list puts "On order" next to "Lasts". Products with a delivery coming need no action, so only rows marked "Nothing" get an Order button. A late delivery shows in red with a button to call the supplier.

  3. One purchase order from many products

    Ticking several low products from the same supplier opens one order with every line filled in. The suggested quantities come with their rule in plain words: enough for about three weeks after delivery, based on the last eight weeks.

  4. Sent the way suppliers already work

    Many suppliers take orders on WhatsApp, so the order goes there as a PDF with the GSTIN, delivery address and expected date. Download and draft are still there for everyone else.

  5. Oldest batch goes out first

    The product page lists batches with their best-before dates and marks the one to pick first. The chart beneath the numbers shows how stock fell and refilled over 90 days against the reorder level.

What I would test next

  1. Give an owner five minutes with the overview and ask which three products to order today. Note whether they reach the answer without opening another screen.
  2. Check the eight-week average around festivals like Diwali, when sales jump. A seasonal adjustment may be needed.
  3. Log how often people change the suggested quantities. Frequent edits would mean the rule needs work.
  4. Test on the 1366 by 768 screens common on office laptops.

Godown is a concept project. Mehta Distributors, its suppliers, customers and all figures are fictional.
